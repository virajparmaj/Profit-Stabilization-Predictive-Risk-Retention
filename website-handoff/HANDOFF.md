## Project question

The platform's original research question is:

> Can low-risk healthcare members be reliably identified from a reduced feature set, and what is the minimum predictive structure needed to stabilise cost-risk segmentation under uncertainty?

This analysis asked a sharper, adjacent question:

> **Healthcare cost is extremely concentrated. Given that, what does a "low-risk member" actually mean — and does identifying one make a pool cheaper, or make it more predictable?**

The findings answer that in six parts: how concentrated the cost is, where averages mislead, which burden signals actually predict the tail, which segments are misread by spend-based rules, and what happens to a pool's risk profile after the model selects it.

## Data

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Source | MEPS HC-251, 2023 full-year consolidated household file (AHRQ) |
| Grain | One row per person-year, calendar year 2023 |
| Raw size | 18,919 rows × 1,374 columns |
| Analysis frame | 18,463 rows with a positive survey weight (`PERWT23F`) |
| Represented population | 334.5M people |
| Primary outcome | `TOTEXP23` — total annual healthcare expenditure, all payers |
| Catastrophic threshold | `$20,000` (the platform's existing `CATA_20K` definition) |
| Weighting | **All population statistics are `PERWT23F`-weighted.** 456 zero-weight rows are excluded |
| Uncertainty | 95% CIs from 4,000 bootstrap resamples, fixed seed 20260824. **Every CI published anywhere in this package is generated by one run and listed in `data/ci-registry.json`** |

**What matters about this data.** It is a **single cross-section**. Panels 27 and 28 are both 2023 observations of different people, not two years of the same people. So there is no within-person cost volatility, no persistence, and no next-year prediction anywhere in this analysis. "Volatility" throughout means **dispersion between members inside a segment** — which is exactly what a pricing pool faces, but is *not* year-over-year movement of an individual.

Two consequences that shape several findings: spending is **censored by access** (people who cannot get care record low spend regardless of health), and chronic conditions are **diagnosed** conditions (under-diagnosis in low-access groups understates their true burden).

**Weighted vs unweighted note.** The platform's existing docs quote an unweighted mean of `$8,422`. This analysis quotes the survey-weighted mean of `$7,487`. Both are correct — one describes the sample, one describes the US population. **The website should use the weighted figures throughout** and should not mix the two.

## Final insights

### Insight 1 — The "average member" does not exist

**Finding.** Annual healthcare spending is so right-skewed that the mean is a tail statistic, not a typical value. The mean sits at the **79th percentile**: 79% of people spend less than the average member. Half of all dollars belong to 5% of members, while the bottom half of the population accounts for under 3% of spending.

**Key numbers.**
- Mean **$7,487** vs median **$1,584** — a **4.7×** gap
- **78.9%** of the population spends below the mean
- Top 5% of members = **48.7%** of all spending; top 1% = **20.1%**; top 10% = **65.2%**
- Bottom 50% of members = **2.9%** of all spending; Gini = **0.781**
- **14.4%** spend $0; **31.7%** spend under $500

**Why it matters.** Every downstream number on this platform — cost saved per low-risk member, segment cost, pricing lanes — is a statement about a mean. In a distribution this skewed, a per-member mean is an artefact of a handful of members and a per-member median is an artefact of the majority who never have a bad year. Reporting either alone is misleading in opposite directions.

**Implication.** Report a **triple** for every segment instead of a single average: median (typical member), mean (budget), and $20k+ exceedance rate (tail exposure).

**Caveat.** Concentration is measured within one year. This data cannot separate members who are expensive every year from members who had one bad year, and that distinction determines how much of this concentration is actionable.

### Insight 2 — Income predicts routine spending, not catastrophic spending

**Finding.** Among insured members under 65, poor and near-poor members have a median cost **62% below** high-income members — but a mean only **18% below**. Splitting spend into routine (first $5,000) and catastrophic (everything above $5,000) shows why: the income gradient lives almost entirely in routine care and nearly vanishes in the tail.

**Key numbers.**
- Median gap **−62%** ($698 vs $1,827) but mean gap only **−18%** ($5,698 vs $6,928)
- Routine spend gap **−25%**; catastrophic spend gap **−14%**
- $20k+ rate **7.1%** [6.1–8.3] vs **8.0%** [7.1–8.8] — under one percentage point apart
- Mean-to-median ratio **8.2×** for poor/near-poor vs **3.8×** for high income

**Why it matters.** It is the cleanest demonstration in this dataset of *why averages mislead*, and it has a direct commercial consequence: a pool of low-income members priced off its median looks like a bargain and is not. Their catastrophic exposure is essentially everyone else's; only their routine consumption is lower — and lower routine consumption among *insured* people is at least partly deferred care rather than better health.

**Implication.** Report the **mean/median ratio** next to every segment cost. A high ratio means the segment's cost is driven by events, not consumption — budget it from the mean and monitor exceedance, never from the median.

**Caveat.** Restricting to insured under-65 removes the Medicare and uninsured confounds but not age or health composition within income bands. Across the *whole* population the income-vs-cost relationship is U-shaped because low-income bands contain many elderly and disabled members — which is itself a caution against reading raw income-vs-cost charts.

### Insight 3 — Counting diagnoses misses tail risk

**Finding.** Functional limitations carry at least as much catastrophic-risk signal per unit as chronic conditions, and they do it at a much lower typical cost. Members with **2 chronic conditions and 2+ functional limitations** have a $20k+ rate statistically indistinguishable from members with **6+ chronic conditions and no limitations** — at a **42% lower median cost**. The pattern holds inside every chronic band.

**Key numbers.**
- Adjusted logistic model (age, sex, mutually adjusted): **OR 1.394** per additional functional limitation vs **OR 1.330** per additional chronic condition
- Pseudo-R² rises **0.151 → 0.172** when limitations are added to a chronic-only model
- 2 chronic + 2+ limitations: **28.7%** [21.0–36.7] over $20k, median **$6,327** (n=195)
- 6+ chronic + 0 limitations: **26.9%** [21.2–32.5] over $20k, median **$10,938** (n=298)

**Why it matters.** This explains why the platform's own model ladder behaves the way it does. The functional-limitation block (B2) contributes the structural signal about who *can* have a bad year; the chronic-count block (B3) contributes the signal about who is expensive on an ordinary day. An AUC measured against a spend-based label rewards the second more visibly — which is how a genuinely important tail marker can look like a modest AUC contributor.

**Implication.** Report clinical burden and functional burden as **two separate axes**, not one summed burden score. A member with 2 conditions and 2 limitations should not be triaged behind a member with 6 conditions and none.

**Caveat.** The two highlighted cells have **overlapping confidence intervals** — the defensible claim is *equivalent* tail risk at lower typical cost, never *higher*. Also, `LIMIT_CT` was built with missing values recoded to "no limitation", which under-counts limitations and therefore **understates** this effect.

### Insight 4 — The cheapest median in the population is also the most emergency-driven

**Finding.** 9.2M people have three or more chronic conditions and **no office-based visits at all**. Their median annual spend of **$377** is the lowest of any segment in this analysis, yet **53.8% of every dollar they do spend goes to the ER or an inpatient bed**, versus 23.8% for chronically ill members who see a doctor. Half of them are labelled "low-risk" by the platform's rule.

**Key numbers.**
- Median **$377** vs **$8,692** for engaged chronic members — a **23× gap on typical cost**, but only a **3× gap on catastrophic rate** (8.4% [5.9–11.1] vs 26.0%)
- **53.8%** of their dollars are ER + inpatient, vs **23.8%** for the engaged group
- Mean **$6,157** = **16× their median**
- **30.1%** have no usual source of care and **11.4%** are uninsured (vs 8.8% and 0.8%); **52.0%** are labelled `LOW_RISK`

**Why it matters.** This is the concrete face of "low spend ≠ low risk". These members are cheap this year, invisible in any spend-ranked report, and the money they do generate arrives through the most expensive door in the system.

**Implication.** Create a standing operational flag — **"3+ chronic conditions and zero ambulatory contact in 12 months"** — as an outreach/care-gap KPI. It is computable from enrolment and claims data alone and needs no model. Exclude this segment from low-risk rating actions until an ambulatory contact is confirmed.

**Caveat.** Zero office visits **mechanically** lowers total spend, so the median gap is partly circular. The non-mechanical results are the ones to quote: the **ER/inpatient share of dollars**, the **access-barrier profile**, and the fact that a group with 3+ diagnoses still has an 8.4% chance of a $20k year while being scored low-risk. This data cannot separate disengagement-by-barrier from disengagement-by-choice.

### Insight 5 — "Low spend" is partly a measure of access, not health

**Finding.** Because the `LOW_RISK` label is built from realised spending, it absorbs anyone who does not consume care — regardless of why. Holding measured disease burden constant, **uninsured members are about five times more likely to be labelled low-risk than insured members**. Only 39% of the resulting cohort is free of both hidden clinical burden and an access barrier.

**Key numbers.**
- Among members with **2+ chronic conditions**: **63.1%** [56.4–69.9] of uninsured are labelled low-risk vs **12.4%** [11.5–13.3] of insured — a **5.1× gap at identical measured burden**
- Overall: **75.4%** of uninsured members are labelled low-risk vs **27.7%** of insured
- Cohort composition: **39.3%** genuinely healthy and engaged · **37.3%** access barrier only · **10.6%** hidden clinical burden only · **12.8%** both
- **40.3%** of the cohort has no usual source of care, vs **17.8%** of everyone else

**Why it matters.** The deployed model is trained to predict this label, so whatever the label conflates, the model inherits. A segmentation that systematically scores uninsured and care-avoidant members as low-risk is a fairness exposure and an adverse-selection exposure at the same time, because those are the members most likely to arrive later and more expensively.

**Implication.** Three concrete changes: (1) require **12-month continuous coverage** for label eligibility, or surface the uninsured share of any low-risk cohort; (2) add a **corroborating condition** to the label — no reported access barrier and a usual source of care; (3) publish the four-way cohort split alongside the headline cohort size so "≈30% low-risk" is never quoted without its composition.

**Caveat.** This is an association in a single cross-section — it shows the label is *contaminated* by coverage, not that coverage causes lower true risk. Some uninsured members genuinely are healthy (they skew young). Because chronic conditions are *diagnosed* conditions and uninsured members are likely under-diagnosed, this is probably an **under-estimate** of the contamination.

### Insight 6 — Risk selection lowers the cost level but makes the pool more tail-concentrated

**Finding.** Selecting the 30% of members the deployed model scores most "low-risk" cuts mean cost by 63% — but the resulting pool is **more** internally unequal than the population it came from, not less. A quarter of the selected pool's expected cost still comes from spending above $20,000.

**Key numbers.**
- Selected top-30%: median **$410** (−74%), mean **$2,779** (−63%)
- But Gini rises **0.781 → 0.827**, worst-1% share of pool spend rises **20.1% → 26.7%**, coefficient of variation rises **2.65 → 3.60**
- **2.9%** of the selected pool still crosses $20,000, and **25.1%** of its expected cost is spend above $20,000
- Tightening to the top 10% makes it worse: worst-1% share **32.6%**, Gini **0.868**
- Reproduced the deployed pipeline out-of-fold: **AUC 0.772** (platform reports 0.773)

**Why it matters.** The platform's stability evidence measures the bootstrap variance of **segment size**. That is a different quantity from **cost variance**. A pool can be perfectly stable in headcount and still be one hospitalisation away from missing its budget. Selection buys a lower expected cost, not a more predictable one.

**Implication.** Add a **cost-dispersion metric** to the Stability & Uncertainty Lab alongside segment size — Gini, CV, and share of expected cost above $20k for each candidate segment. Any pricing built on this segmentation still needs stop-loss sized to the *selected* pool's tail, which is proportionally fatter than the population's.

**Caveat.** Out-of-fold prediction on a single year predicts a **same-year** label; it is not a forward-looking risk score. The selected pool's low median is partly definitional since the label is built from spend — the Gini/CV/concentration comparison is the part that is not.

## What the data collectively tells us

Use this as the synthesis section — it should not repeat the charts.

Healthcare cost is not a quantity you can average. It is a **rare-event process wearing the costume of a continuous variable**: most members generate almost nothing, a few generate almost everything, and the mean belongs to the second group while describing the first. Once that is accepted, three things follow that this analysis found empirically.

First, **most of the segment differences that look large are differences in routine consumption, not in tail exposure.** Income moves the median by 62% and the tail by 14%. That means a lot of conventional segmentation is sorting members by how much ordinary care they use, not by how likely they are to have an expensive year.

Second, **the signals that predict the tail are not the signals that predict the level.** Functional limitation is a better per-unit tail marker than diagnosis count, and ambulatory disengagement changes the *shape* of a member's spending — from managed to emergency — without changing its size until it does.

Third, and most importantly for this platform: **selecting low-risk members reduces expected cost without reducing relative volatility.** The selected pool is cheaper and *more* concentrated. So "low-risk segmentation" is a margin lever, not a stability lever — and the label being used to define it is measuring access as much as health. The honest version of the platform's own result is: the model reliably finds members who were cheap this year, roughly a third of whom were cheap because they could not get care.

## Methodology

Only what an interviewer would reasonably ask about.

- **Survey weighting.** All population estimates use `PERWT23F`. Weighted quantiles use the standard `(cumulative weight − 0.5 × weight) / total weight` interpolation. 456 zero-weight rows are excluded from population estimates.
- **Concentration.** Lorenz-style curves computed by sorting members most-expensive-first and accumulating weighted spend. Gini computed by trapezoidal integration of the weighted Lorenz curve.
- **Tail metrics.** Three complementary measures are used rather than one: `$20k+` exceedance rate (how often), share of expected cost above `$20k` (how much of the budget), and the share of a pool's own spend held by its worst 1% (how concentrated).
- **Routine vs catastrophic decomposition.** Each member's spend is split into `min(spend, $5,000)` and `max(spend − $5,000, 0)`, then averaged within a segment. This isolates whether a segment's cost gap lives in ordinary consumption or in rare events.
- **Confounding control by restriction, not reweighting.** The income comparison is restricted to insured members under 65 so it cannot be driven by Medicare enrolment or by uninsured non-utilisation.
- **Burden regression.** Logistic regression, outcome `TOTEXP23 ≥ $20,000`, covariates: age/10, female, chronic-condition count, functional-limitation count. Odds ratios are mutually adjusted. n = 18,463.
- **Model reproduction.** The deployed `low_risk_model_B3_chronic_xgb.joblib` pipeline was re-fit under stratified 5-fold cross-validation to produce honest out-of-fold probabilities (AUC 0.772 vs the platform's reported 0.773). No in-sample scores were used for any claim.
- **Uncertainty.** Percentile bootstrap, 4,000 resamples of the underlying records, single fixed seed (20260824). Every published interval is emitted by one generator run and recorded in `data/ci-registry.json`, which is the canonical source; `analysis/src/check_consistency.py` verifies that the data files, the metric dump and both markdown documents agree with it. Heatmap cells with n < 40 are suppressed.
- **Robustness check.** MEPS panels 27 and 28 were compared for a survey-tenure artefact and none was found (median $1,432 vs $1,685; mean chronic count 2.01 vs 2.12), so no finding here is a panel-composition effect.

## Limitations

These must remain visible on the page.

1. **Single year, cross-sectional.** MEPS 2023 only. No within-person cost volatility, persistence, churn, or next-year prediction. "Volatility" means dispersion *between members inside a segment*, not movement of an individual over time.
2. **Association, not causation.** Nothing here identifies a causal effect. Spending is jointly determined by illness, coverage, price and access.
3. **Spending is censored by access.** Observed spend understates true risk for members who cannot obtain care. This is the mechanism behind Insight 5 and it limits how far any low-spend metric can be read as "healthy".
4. **Survey data with survey weights.** Estimates are `PERWT23F`-weighted to 334.5M people. Model evaluation is record-level and **not** survey-weighted — the two must never be quoted as the same kind of statistic.
5. **`LOW_RISK` is an operational label, not a clinical judgement.** It is bottom-30% spend ($484) plus zero ER visits plus zero inpatient stays. Not a diagnosis, not a guarantee of future cost.
6. **Small cells.** Heatmap cells with n < 40 are suppressed. The 2-chronic/2+-limitation vs 6+-chronic/0-limitation comparison has overlapping CIs and must be described as *equivalent* tail risk, never *higher*.
7. **Functional limitations are under-counted** — missing values were recoded to "no limitation" during preprocessing, which biases the Insight 3 effect downward.
8. **Self-reported utilisation.** MEPS expenditures are survey-collected and provider-verified, not claims. Extreme values carry measurement error.

## What I would investigate next

Four specific follow-up questions, in priority order.

1. **How persistent is the tail?** Link two consecutive MEPS panel years for the same people and measure what share of the top 1% in year 1 remains in the top 1% in year 2. This is the single biggest open question — if catastrophic spend is highly persistent, "the tail is unpredictable" is too strong and much of it is forecastable from prior-year spend.
2. **Does the disengaged-chronic segment convert?** Follow members with 3+ chronic conditions and zero ambulatory contact into the following year and measure their catastrophic rate. This is what would turn Insight 4 from a striking cross-sectional pattern into an actionable outreach case with an expected ROI.
3. **What does a coverage-corrected label do to the result?** Re-derive `LOW_RISK` with a 12-month continuous-coverage requirement and re-run the full model ladder. Quantify how much the cohort size, the AUC, and the cohort's composition change. That directly tests whether the platform's headline result survives Insight 5.
4. **Is the routine-care income gap a cost-sharing effect?** Among insured members only, decompose the −25% routine-spend gap by plan generosity and out-of-pocket share. If it is a deductible effect rather than a health effect, it is modifiable — and it changes what the gap means commercially.
